## Abstract

Productivity growth in Latin America has remained persistently weak, and in Peru average total factor productivity (TFP) growth has averaged only around 0.1% per year over the past decade. This thesis studies whether the 2017 El Niño event — which caused severe flooding along Peru's coast and drought in the southern highlands — had a negative effect on firm-level TFP. Combining firm panel data from 2012 to 2020 with geographic differences in rainfall anomalies, I apply a two-way fixed-effects difference-in-differences design that compares firms in districts hit by heavy rainfall (60mm or more) to those in districts with little to no anomaly (0-30mm). Results show that El Niño 2017 lowered firm-level TFP by roughly 12 to 14 percent, a finding that holds at the 1% significance level across alternative productivity measures and machine-learning-based estimators. In the short run, the effect is strongest among small firms and in the commerce and services sectors, linked to falling demand and declines in worker health, while manufacturing firms show a more persistent drop that lasts through 2019 as infrastructure is rebuilt. Firms affected by the shock also reduced spending on employee training and employed a smaller share of college-educated workers. Looking further back, the 1998 El Niño event is similarly associated with lower long-run firm-level labor productivity.
